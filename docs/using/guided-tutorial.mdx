<KeyIdea>
The console carries a **guided tutorial** that teaches the platform from inside the platform.
It highlights the real buttons on the real screens, waits while you click them, and leaves
you holding <span className="hl">a small working model you built yourself</span> rather than
a demo somebody showed you.

It is the fastest way to take a new colleague from "what is this" to a data set, a resource
network, a measurement and an event, in about the time a presentation would have taken.

It comes as two tours. **Build a network from scratch** has you make a small model yourself.
**Explore a demo site** builds a small industrial site for you and follows one incident through
it: a pump's bearing runs hot, an alarm arrives, a work permit is raised and worked, the fault is
fixed. Same engine, same real screens; one teaches by building, the other by reading.
</KeyIdea>

## Starting it

Two entry points, both leading to the same flow:

- **The user menu, top right.** The entry marked **Guided Tutorial**, available on every
  page of the console.
- **The home screen.** The "New to DataHub?" bar, for anyone who has just signed in for the
  first time.

Either one opens **Start guided tutorial?**, which offers three ways in:

- **Build a network from scratch**, the full build tour described in the next sections.
- **Explore a demo site**, the demo tour, described [below](#explore-a-demo-site).
- **Or jump to a chapter of the build tour**, which plays from the chapter you pick through to
  the end. If the chapter you choose needs something an earlier one would have built, the
  tutorial creates that first rather than leaving you stuck.

## Pick the industry it speaks in

Before the first step you choose a scenario: **Oil & Gas** (the default), **Tech / IoT**,
**Manufacturing** or **Energy**.

The choice changes the vocabulary rather than the mechanics, in both tours. The data set you
create, the resources you connect and the measurement you add are all named for the industry you picked,
so the model you finish with reads like a small piece of your own operation. That matters
more than it sounds: <span className="t-brand">the point of the tour is to recognise your own
plant in it</span>, and generic example names are what usually stop people doing that.

## The six chapters

Each chapter opens with a short panel explaining why the thing exists before anything gets
clicked, then walks you through building it.

| Chapter | What you do | What you are left with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Datasets** | Create a [data set](/using/datasets), name it, give it an external id | The boundary everything else in the tour lands inside |
| **Resources** | Build a small network of [resources](/using/resources) and connect them | The graph, in miniature, with your own relationships on it |
| **Timeseries** | Add a [measurement](/using/timeseries) and attach it to a resource | A series that hangs off a thing rather than floating alone |
| **Correlation** | Look at your series alongside two siblings in [Insights](/using/insights) | The point that one signal is rarely the story |
| **Events** | Record what happened to the asset as [events](/using/events) | Readings and happenings tied to the same network |
| **Build a network** | A larger network is built for you, highlighted and explained, then handed over for you to extend | A model you can keep and carry on with |

The order is deliberate and it is the same order the rest of this section is written in:
a container, the things in it, what they measure, how those measurements relate, what
happened to them, and then the whole shape at once.

## Explore a demo site

The demo tour is for the person who wants to see the platform holding a story together before
modelling anything. Pick an industry and it seeds a site for you: a tank, two valves, a pump and
its standby, a motor, and three signals on the pump, with two days of readings and an alarm
already in the log. Then it follows one incident in six short chapters:

| Chapter | What you do | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The site** | Open the site from the resource list and read its graph | Equipment, the process flow between it, and the pump's signals as nodes of the same graph |
| **The signal** | Chart the bearing temperature in [Insights](/using/insights), zoom in, overlay the discharge pressure | Two readings from one machine on one chart |
| **The alarm** | Open the alarm in the [event log](/using/events) | An alarm is a record tied to the pump, with its source and threshold |
| **The permit** | Watch a work permit arrive and open it; narrow the log to its external id | A permit's life as a trail of events sharing one external id: open, in progress, closed |
| **The fix** | See the repair posted, the signal settle, and the home page count the alarm | Readings and happenings on one timeline |
| **What's next** | Read what is planned | Anomaly and event detection on the same foundation |

Everything you click is a real console action; the systems around DataHub (condition
monitoring, the permit system, the technician) are played by the tour posting real events and
readings as the story moves on. Nothing is typed for you except where the point of a step is to
type. It takes about five minutes.

The demo creates the same kind of real data as the build tour: a root resource with six pieces
of equipment under it, three time series, their readings, and the events of the story, all under
external ids that start with `demo_` and a marker unique to the run. Labels (SITE, PUMP, MOTOR,
VALVE, TANK) and relationship types (CONTAINS, FEEDS, DRIVES, MEASURES) are created only where
the tenant lacks them, and are left in place afterwards as ordinary vocabulary. The last step
offers **Keep it** or **Remove tutorial data**, exactly as the build tour does. It has no
chapters to jump into, because every chapter depends on the site the first one builds.

## How it behaves while it runs

- **It waits for you.** A step with an action does not advance until you actually perform it,
  clicking the highlighted button or typing a name of at least a few characters. Nothing is
  filled in on your behalf where the point of the step is to do it yourself.
- **It blocks everything else.** While a step is live, clicks outside the highlighted area
  are swallowed, so it is difficult to wander off mid-step and end up lost. Where a step
  deliberately hands you the canvas, the canvas becomes clickable again.
- **It follows you across screens.** Chapters run on the data sets, resources, time series,
  insights and events pages, and the tutorial navigates between them and picks up where it
  left off.
- **Progress is a chapter strip** at the top of each bubble, so you can see how much of the
  journey is behind you rather than counting steps.
- **You can leave and come back.** Every bubble carries **Back**, **Quit** and **Next**, and
  some carry **Skip**. If you quit or navigate away mid-tour, the next page you open offers a
  banner with **Resume**, **Restart** and **Quit**.
- **It speaks the console's language.** English and Norwegian Bokmål, following the
  [language setting](/using/console-tour#things-in-the-top-bar) rather than a separate
  control.

## It creates real data, and tidies up after itself

This is the part worth knowing before turning a team loose on a production tenant.

<span className="t-accent">What the tutorial creates is real.</span> In the build tour: a data
set, resources, a time series with datapoints, a couple of events, and two extra series for the
correlation chapter. In the demo tour: a site of seven resources, three series, their readings
and the events of the story. All ordinary objects in your tenant, visible to anybody who can
read them. Nothing is a simulation or a sandbox mode.

Three behaviours follow from that, and they are all deliberate:

- **The last step asks.** It offers **Keep it**, which leaves the network in place to inspect
  or carry on with, or **Remove tutorial data**, which deletes the data set, resources,
  series and events this run created. Labels and relationship types the demo tour added stay.
- **Starting again cleans up first.** A fresh run deletes the previous run's objects before
  it begins, so repeated runs by the same person do not pile up.
- **Each run gets its own identifiers.** A run stamps a unique marker so the things it creates get
  unique [external ids](/concepts/naming-and-standards#external-ids-are-promises) rather than
  colliding with the last run's.

Deletion is best effort: if something cannot be removed, the tutorial carries on rather than
stopping to complain. In a shared tenant it is worth a glance at the data set list afterwards.

:::tip Where to run it
A test tenant if you have one. If you do not, it is safe enough in a real one provided people
know to finish with **Remove tutorial data**, and provided the tutorial data set is not left
sitting in a list of production data sets long enough for somebody to mistake it for real.
:::

## What it needs

- **Permission to create a data set**, which is the all-data-sets grant rather than a grant
  on individual data sets, because a data set is the unit access is granted on. Without it
  the first chapter cannot complete.
  [How permissions work →](/administration/dataset-permissions)
- **The feature enabled in the deployment.** It is on by default; an administrator can switch it
  off for everyone in the console's configuration, which also removes the menu entry and the
  home-screen bar.
- **For the demo tour, the whole platform running.** Its readings and events reach the screens
  through the message bus and the consumer, and its graph through the mirror; on a deployment
  where those are down the tour waits up to a minute at two points and then carries on with
  whatever has arrived.

## When to reach for it, and when not

**Reach for it** on somebody's first day, for a team that has read about the platform and not
touched it, and as the fastest way to answer "what does this actually look like" without
booking a demonstration.

**Read [your first hour](/start/first-hour) instead** if you would rather work from written
steps you can read ahead in, skim, or follow while building something of your own. It covers
the same journey in the same order, which means the two can be mixed: take the tour, then use
the written version when you build the real thing.
