<KeyIdea>
The platform is free software under the GNU AGPL-3.0, and that is the whole product: one
codebase, one feature set, nothing held back for a paid edition. You can run it, change it and
keep it inside your organisation without owing anyone the source of your changes.

The obligation starts at the edge of your organisation. When a modified version reaches
someone outside, as a copy handed to a partner or as a service outsiders use over the
network, you must offer them the source of your changes. <span className="hl">Sharing is not
the violation; sharing without offering the source is.</span>

An enterprise licence from IntelliStream lifts that one obligation. It changes the terms,
not the software.
</KeyIdea>

## What is licensed, and how

The platform is under the GNU Affero General Public License, "either version 3 of the
License, or (at your option) any later version", as the repository's `NOTICE` file states.

The client libraries are the deliberate exception. In the platform repository,
`datahub-api-model`, the small library that defines the API's data shapes, and
`datahub-java-sdk` each carry the Apache License 2.0: their own `LICENSE` file, an
`SPDX-License-Identifier: Apache-2.0` header on every source file, and the same licence in
the metadata they are published with. The comment in the build file says why: "A client
library has to be safe to link into someone else's application, and a copyleft one is not."
The Python and Rust SDKs are Apache-2.0 on the same principle.
[The licence, in the FAQ →](/reference/faq#what-is-the-licence)

There is one codebase. The version you clone from GitHub, the version IntelliStream hosts for
customers and the version covered by an enterprise licence are the same software with the same
features. Nothing in the platform checks a licence, calls home or expires.

## What you can do without asking anyone

The AGPL is a copyright licence, so it only has something to say when you do what copyright
law would otherwise forbid: copy, adapt, distribute. Using the software is not one of those
things.

- **Run it, for any purpose, at any scale.** Section 2 "explicitly affirms your unlimited
  permission to run the unmodified Program". No user limit, no tag limit, no tenant limit, no
  field of use.
- **Modify it and run the modified version for yourself.** Section 2 continues: "You may
  make, run and propagate covered works that you do not convey, without conditions". A change
  that stays inside your organisation is covered by that sentence.
- **Keep your data, your model and your configuration private.** They are not the Program.
  Section 2 says the output of running a covered work is covered "only if the output, given
  its content, constitutes a covered work", which your measurements, your asset model and
  your reports do not.
- **Have a contractor do the work for you.** Section 2 lets you give the software to others
  "for the sole purpose of having them make modifications exclusively for you, or provide you
  with facilities for running those works", provided they act on your behalf, under your
  direction and control, and on terms that forbid them keeping copies outside the
  relationship. A systems integrator patching it for you, or a hosting company running your
  instance, fits that paragraph if the contract does.
- **Pass on unmodified copies.** Section 4 allows verbatim copies, with the copyright and
  licence notices intact and a copy of the licence alongside, and adds that "you may charge
  any price or no price for each copy".
- **Read it before you accept anything.** Section 9: "You are not required to accept this
  License in order to receive or run a copy of the Program." Acceptance is only implied when
  you modify or propagate it.

## Modifying the platform versus building on it

Section 0 defines the terms. To "modify" is "to copy from or adapt all or part of the work in
a fashion requiring copyright permission, other than the making of an exact copy", and the
result is a "modified version". A "covered work" is the unmodified Program or a work based on
it. Every obligation on this page attaches to covered works and to nothing else, so the first
question is always whether what you built is one.

**Building on the platform through its API is not modifying it.** An application that talks
to DataHub over the REST API, through one of the SDKs, over MCP or through a
[subscription](/using/subscriptions) is a separate program. It contains none of the
platform's code. The only DataHub code it links is the client library, and that library is
Apache-2.0 precisely so the application can be <span className="t-brand">your own work,
under your own terms</span>: closed, sold, hosted or given away as you choose. The AGPL
obligations attach to modifying the platform itself and then conveying it or serving it over
a network, and not to the programs that call it. This is the normal way to extend DataHub,
and it is why the SDKs exist.

**Patching the platform's own code is modifying it.** A change to the API, the console, the
consumers or the housekeeping service, a fork with a feature added, a rebuilt image with a
behaviour changed: each of these produces a modified version, and the rest of this page
applies to it.

**Anything that embeds platform code beyond the SDKs is a legal judgement.** Section 1 draws
the Corresponding Source line at "shared libraries and dynamically linked subprograms that the
work is specifically designed to require, such as by intimate data communication or control
flow", and section 5 says an "aggregate", separate and independent works shipped side by side
on the same medium, does not pull the other works under the licence. Code compiled into the
platform's process, a plugin it loads, a copy of an AGPL module inside your own service, or a
component the platform cannot start without, sits closer to the first description than to
the second. If your design depends on where that line falls, ask counsel before relying on it
rather than after.

:::tip A good test
Could you delete the platform's source tree and still build your application? If yes, you
built on it. If your build breaks, you have modified it or combined with it, and the rest of
this page is for you.
:::

## The three situations

The licence has two triggers. **Conveying**, defined in section 0 as any propagation "that
enables other parties to make or receive copies", is what sections 4, 5 and 6 govern.
**Remote network interaction** with a modified version is what section 13 adds, and it is the
clause that makes the AGPL different from the ordinary GPL. Section 0 is explicit that "mere
interaction with a user through a computer network, with no transfer of a copy, is not
conveying", which is exactly why section 13 exists.

Put the two together and every deployment lands in one of three situations.

| Situation | Example | What the licence asks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Inside your organisation** | Your engineers patch the platform and your staff use it, on your servers or in your cloud | Nothing. You are not conveying, and the only network users are your own, inside the organisation that already holds the source |
| **A copy leaves** | You hand a modified build to a partner, a customer or a supplier, or ship it inside a product | Sections 4, 5 and 6: keep the notices, mark what you changed and when, license the whole work under the AGPL, and give the recipient the Corresponding Source or a written offer of it |
| **Outsiders use it over the network** | People outside your organisation, clients, partners or the public, use your modified version through the console or the API | Section 13: the modified version must prominently offer those users the Corresponding Source of your version, from a network server at no charge |

Two things about the table are worth reading twice.

Section 13 says "all users", not outside users. Your own staff are users too, and the
obligation towards them is met trivially: the organisation that made the change already holds
its source. The clause bites when the users are outside, because they hold nothing.

<span className="t-brand">The line is the edge of your organisation, not the edge of your
servers.</span> A modified version in your own cloud account, used only by your staff, is
inside. The same version on your own hardware, used by a client, is outside. Where the edge of
your organisation runs is a question of law and of corporate structure, not of software: a
wholly owned subsidiary, a joint venture, a shared-services company, a franchisee and a
long-term contractor are all different cases, and the licence text does not decide them for
you. Ask counsel, and if the answer is uncertain, the [enterprise licence](#the-enterprise-licence)
removes the question rather than answering it.

## What "offer the Corresponding Source" means in practice

Section 1 defines Corresponding Source as "all the source code needed to generate, install,
and (for an executable work) run the object code and to modify the work, including scripts to
control those activities". It excludes System Libraries and general-purpose tools "used
unmodified" that are not part of the work, and it need not include "anything that users can
regenerate automatically from other parts of the Corresponding Source".

For a modified DataHub that means, concretely:

- **The platform source with your changes applied**, not a diff on its own and not a
  description of the change. The simplest form is a fork of the repository with your commits
  on it.
- **The build**, which in this repository is the Gradle build and the container build the
  launcher uses. If you changed either, the changed version is part of the source. If you did
  not, the upstream one already is.
- **Deployment scripts, where they are needed to install or run the modified work.** Section
  1 includes "scripts to control those activities". Your secrets, certificates, tenant
  registry and data are not source and are not owed to anyone.
- **Nothing you did not change and did not combine.** The backing stores, the JVM, the
  operating system and the container runtime are System Libraries or generally available
  programs used unmodified.

Section 5 adds what a modified source version must say about itself: "prominent notices
stating that you modified it, and giving a relevant date" (5a), a notice that it is released
under this licence (5b), and the whole work licensed under the AGPL "to anyone who comes into
possession of a copy" (5c). A changes file, or the commit history of a public fork, satisfies
the first two. The third is the part people underestimate: once a modified version is
conveyed, the recipient holds it under the AGPL and may pass it on, and section 10 says you
"may not impose any further restrictions" on top.

Section 6 lists the ways to deliver source with a binary. The ones that matter here are a copy
alongside the binary (6a), a written offer valid for at least three years to supply it on
request (6b), or, when the binary is downloaded from somewhere, "equivalent access to the
Corresponding Source in the same way through the same place at no further charge" (6d). A
repository the recipient can clone, or a source tarball next to the image, meets 6d. The
source must be in a publicly documented format and "require no special password or key for
unpacking, reading or copying".

Section 13 is the operational one for a hosted deployment. The modified version must
"prominently offer all users interacting with it remotely through a computer network" an
opportunity to receive its Corresponding Source "from a network server at no charge, through
some standard or customary means". The console already carries that offer: the **About
DataHub** entry in the user menu links to the source, the licence text and the third-party
notices, and the comment on that template says why it is there. <span className="hl">If you
modify the platform and outsiders use it, point that link at the source of your version</span>,
a public fork or a tarball on a server they can reach, and leave it where users can find it.
Removing the offer from a modified, network-served version without an equivalent is the
violation section 13 describes.

:::tip A test that takes ten minutes
Give the archive or repository you intend to offer to an engineer who has never seen your
changes, on a clean machine, and ask them to run the install command from
[Installing](/administration/installing#installing-the-evaluation-stack). If it builds and
starts, it is Corresponding Source. If they have to ask you for something, that something
belongs in the offer.
:::

## Contributing upstream is the simplest way to comply

Every obligation above exists to keep improvements to the platform available to its users.
The least effort way to meet it is to give the improvement to the platform.

A change merged upstream has no separate source to offer, because it is in the source everyone
already gets. It also stops being yours to maintain: it is rebuilt, tested and shipped with
every release instead of being rebased by your team on every upgrade, security advisories
cover it, and the people who wrote the surrounding code review it before it lands. For a fix
or a feature that is not a competitive secret, which describes most of them, this is strictly
better than a private patch.

The repository's contributing guide is short. Contributions are licensed under the same
AGPL-3.0 terms, and there is no contributor licence agreement to sign; instead every commit
carries a `Signed-off-by:` trailer under the
[Developer Certificate of Origin](https://developercertificate.org/), which `git commit -s`
adds for you. Fork, branch, sign off, open a pull request, and open an issue first if the
change is large enough that you would rather know it is wanted before building it.
[The repository →](https://github.com/IntelliStream-DataHub/datahub-platform)

## The enterprise licence

Some organisations need to keep their improvements private while people outside use them: a
service company whose modified platform is its product, a vendor shipping it inside
equipment, a group whose corporate edge is too complicated to draw. For them IntelliStream
offers an **enterprise licence**.

**What it does.** It lifts the source-sharing obligation for your own improvements. Conveying
a modified version to a partner or customer, or running it as a service for outsiders, no
longer requires offering them the source of your changes.

**What it does not do.** It does not change the software. There is no enterprise edition, no
extra feature and no held-back component: the licensed version and the open-source version
are the same code, built from the same repository, with the same features. The licence exists
only for those who need it, and it is quoted per deployment, in writing, once the deployment
is known. [Talk to us →](https://intellistream.ai/contact-us)

The licence text anticipates the arrangement. Section 5(c) says the AGPL "gives no permission
to license the work in any other way, but it does not invalidate such permission if you have
separately received it", and section 7 lets a copyright holder attach additional permissions.
An enterprise licence is that separately received permission, granted by IntelliStream as the
platform's copyright holder.

| You | Enterprise licence |
| --- | --- |
| Run the platform, modified or not, for your own organisation | Not needed |
| Build applications on the API and SDKs, internal or commercial | Not needed |
| Contribute your changes upstream | Not needed |
| Convey an unmodified copy to a partner | Not needed, section 4 already allows it |
| Convey a modified version to a partner or customer and keep the changes private | Needed |
| Host a modified version that outsiders use, and keep the changes private | Needed |
| Cannot say with confidence where your organisation's edge runs | Worth discussing; it removes the question |

## Do we have to publish our changes?

Worked examples, in the order people tend to ask them. Each answer is the licence applied to a
plain case; the moment a case stops being plain, it belongs to counsel.

- **A plant patches the console to show a local unit convention and uses it internally.**
  No. Section 2: no conveying, no outside users. Consider contributing it anyway.
- **An integrator modifies the platform under contract, for you, on your servers.** No,
  provided the contract meets section 2: exclusively on your behalf, under your direction and
  control, no copies kept outside the relationship. Whether a particular contract does is a
  judgement.
- **A service company hosts DataHub for its clients, unmodified.** Nothing beyond the offer
  already in the console, because section 13 applies "if you modify the Program". Leave the
  About dialog's source link in place.
- **The same service company adds features and hosts the result for its clients.** Yes. The
  clients interact with a modified version over the network, so section 13 applies: offer
  them the Corresponding Source of that version, or hold an enterprise licence.
- **A vendor ships DataHub inside a product it sells, with changes.** Yes. Delivering the
  product conveys the modified version, so sections 5 and 6 apply and the source, or a written
  offer of it, travels with the product. Section 6 has extra rules for a "User Product",
  consumer goods and things built into dwellings, and says doubtful cases are resolved in
  favour of coverage; industrial equipment usually is not one, but have that checked. An
  enterprise licence lifts the source obligation here too.
- **A vendor ships DataHub unmodified alongside its own software.** Conveying an unmodified
  copy is section 4: keep the notices, include the licence. Section 5's aggregate paragraph
  says shipping separate works side by side does not bring the vendor's own software under
  the AGPL. Whether the two are separate works or one combined program is the linking
  question above, and a judgement.
- **A company sells an application that uses the API and the Python SDK.** No. It is a
  separate work built on Apache-2.0 libraries, and the platform's licence does not attach to
  it.
- **A wholly owned subsidiary runs the parent's modified version, or a joint venture runs
  one deployment for its owners.** This is the "your organisation" question. Separate legal
  entities are the case the licence text does not settle, and the honest answer is that it
  depends on your structure and your jurisdiction. Ask counsel, or take the enterprise
  licence and stop thinking about it.
- **Your company is acquired, or spins a division out.** Section 10 covers an "entity
  transaction": each party receiving a copy also receives the licences the predecessor had,
  plus a right to the Corresponding Source from them. An enterprise licence, if you hold one,
  is a contract with its own terms; read those.

## Frequently asked

### Is there an enterprise edition with more features?

No, and that is deliberate. One platform, one codebase, one set of features. The enterprise
licence changes the terms your own improvements are held under, nothing else. If you never
need that, the open-source version is the whole product.

### Do we have to publish our asset model, our data or our configuration?

No. None of them is the Program. The licence covers the platform's code and works based on
it; what you put into a running instance, and what comes out, is yours.

### We only use the API and the SDKs. Does any of this apply to us?

Not to your application. It is your own work under your own terms, built on Apache-2.0
client libraries. The platform it talks to remains under the AGPL, and whoever runs that
platform carries the obligations on this page, which for an unmodified platform amount to
leaving the source offer in the console where it is.

### Does the platform check a licence key or call home?

No. Nothing in the platform requires an outbound connection at runtime: no licence check, no
telemetry, no hosted dependency. [Security →](/administration/security#auditability-of-the-platform-itself)

### Can we remove the About dialog from the console?

Removing it is itself a modification, and the modified version would then lack the offer
section 13 requires of any modified version people use over a network. Keep it, or replace it
with an equivalent that points at the source of your version.

### What happens if we get it wrong?

Section 8 says your rights under the licence end automatically if you propagate or modify
outside its terms, and it also says how they come back: stop the violation and the licence is
reinstated, permanently if it is the first notice you have had from that copyright holder and
you cure within 30 days of receiving it. The practical version: fix it when told, promptly.

### Is there a warranty?

Not from the licence. Sections 15 and 16 disclaim warranty and liability "except when
otherwise stated in writing". Commitments about response and availability, where they exist,
come from a written support or hosting agreement, not from the AGPL.
